Introduction

Colour as a Continuous Phenomenon

Colour accompanies human culture across radically different historical and technological environments. It appears simultaneously as a physical phenomenon, a perceptual experience, an artistic medium and a form of technological information.

The book follows the transformation of colour through these different domains, from natural phenomena and painting to textiles, printing, digital displays and computational systems.

From Property to Principle

The central historical movement examined in the book is the gradual emergence of colour as an autonomous organising principle.

The trajectory begins with Newton's investigation of light, continues through Goethe and Chevreul's investigations of perception and simultaneous contrast, and reaches Sonia Delaunay's Simultanism.

From there, the same structural logic is followed into textile design, digital raster systems and contemporary generative art.

A Continuous Visual Logic

The book proposes that these fields should not be understood as isolated historical phenomena. Instead, they form successive stages of a common mode of visual thought.

Each stage preserves and transforms principles developed by the preceding one, progressively shifting colour from material property toward relational structure and eventually executable mathematical process.

Chapter 1

The Scientific Foundations of Modern Colour Theory

Newton Light Spectrum

Newton and the Structure of Light

The scientific transformation of modern colour theory begins with Isaac Newton's Opticks, published in 1704.

Newton's prism experiments demonstrated that white light could be separated into a continuous spectrum. The prism did not create colour; it spatially separated components already present within white light.

The second-prism experiment and the recombination of the spectrum further established the composite character of white light and helped move colour into the domain of experimentally measurable natural phenomena.

Goethe Perception

From Optics to Perception

Goethe's Zur Farbenlehre, published in 1810, redirected the investigation toward the experience of colour and the role of the perceiving subject.

Colour was understood not simply as an objective property of light but as an event arising through the interaction between light, darkness, the visual environment and the perceiving organism.

Goethe's discussion of afterimages and coloured shadows emphasized the active role of the eye in the organisation of visual experience.

Chevreul Simultaneous Contrast

Colour as a Relational Phenomenon

Michel Eugène Chevreul's practical work at the Gobelins Manufactory led to a systematic investigation of simultaneous colour contrast.

The same thread could appear different depending on the colours surrounding it. This demonstrated that perceived colour could not be understood independently from its visual environment.

Chevreul's theory established colour as a relational quality: perception continuously compares neighbouring colours, modifying their apparent intensity, lightness and hue.

Neo-Impressionism Seurat

From Theory to Painting

Neo-Impressionism transformed scientific colour theory into a practical pictorial method.

Georges Seurat separated pure colours into small areas rather than relying entirely on material pigment mixtures. Their visual unity was produced through perception at an appropriate viewing distance.

The painting therefore became a carefully organised visual field in which the operation of perception itself participated in the production of chromatic unity.

The Turning Point

By the end of the nineteenth century, colour had moved beyond the status of an isolated physical or descriptive property.

It had become an active component of visual organisation whose effects depended upon relationships, interaction and perception.

This transformation established the conceptual conditions for the emergence of Simultanism and the autonomy of colour explored in the following chapter.

Chapter 2

Simultanism and the Autonomy of Colour

Orphism Simultanism

Colour Becomes Structure

In the work of Robert and Sonia Delaunay, colour increasingly became independent of traditional representational constraints.

Within the context of Orphism, pictorial unity could arise from the rhythmic interaction of colours rather than from recognisable objects or conventional perspective.

Simultanism developed this principle into a comprehensive compositional system.

Sonia Delaunay and Simultanism

For Sonia Delaunay, simultaneous contrast was no longer merely a phenomenon to be observed. It became an organising logic for the entire image.

Colours activate one another through juxtaposition. The resulting visual field is dynamic rather than static, because every colour simultaneously influences and is influenced by the colours surrounding it.

Colour therefore becomes an autonomous spatial force capable of generating movement, rhythm and visual experience.

1912–1913 Contrastes simultanés

Contrastes simultanés

In the Contrastes simultanés series, created between 1912 and 1913, scientific colour theory is transformed directly into a creative compositional principle.

Intensely coloured fields are placed in direct juxtaposition. Complementary relationships reinforce the intensity of neighbouring colours and keep the visual surface in a state of tension.

Geometric forms become supporting structures for chromatic relations rather than independent bearers of meaning.

1913 Bal Bullier

Rhythm and Movement

In Bal Bullier, the experience of modern urban life is transformed into a dynamic chromatic environment.

Music, dance, artificial light and bodily movement are expressed through interwoven colour fields rather than through narrative representation alone.

Curved forms and successive colour relationships create an impression of continuous rotation and visual flow.

1914 Prismes électriques

Prismes électriques

In Prismes électriques, the visual experience of modern artificial light becomes increasingly detached from direct representation.

Concentric circles, arcs and colour segments establish a modular structure in which colour, geometry and rhythm operate as a unified compositional system.

The circle becomes a model of simultaneous vision: chromatic relationships radiate through the composition while the viewer's gaze continually expands, contracts and returns toward the centre.

Chapter 3

Structural Systems of Colour

Textile Gobelins

Colour as a System

The structural interpretation of colour extends from painting into textile production, where colours had to be classified, compared and reproduced consistently.

At the Gobelins Manufactory, Chevreul developed systems for organising dyed yarns according to relationships of tone, lightness and saturation.

Colour consequently became not only an aesthetic quality but a structural element within a regulated production system.

Atelier Simultané Textile Design

Delaunay Beyond Painting

During the 1920s, Sonia Delaunay extended the principles of Simultanism into textile and dress design through the Atelier Simultané.

Fabric became an active chromatic field. Seams, folds, pleats and the movement of the body continually changed the relationships between adjacent colour surfaces.

The composition therefore became spatial and dynamic rather than a fixed image on a static plane.

Modular Pattern

Diamonds, zigzags, bands, arcs and other geometric units function as structural modules within textile compositions.

Their significance does not exist independently. It emerges through repetition, variation, orientation and chromatic relationships with neighbouring units.

Repetition therefore becomes a form of rhythmic variation rather than mechanical duplication.

Jacquard 1804 Pattern Logic

The Jacquard Loom

The structural logic of textile patterning is connected in the book to Joseph Marie Jacquard's loom, patented in 1804.

Perforated cards encoded sequences of instructions controlling the movement of warp threads. Pattern therefore became a predetermined sequence of discrete operations.

This mechanism demonstrated how complex visual forms could emerge from the consistent application of relatively simple structural rules.

From Textile Matrix to Digital Raster

The woven textile can be understood as an analogue visual matrix in which each thread intersection functions as an elementary unit of information.

Geometry, repetition, displacement, rotation and chromatic alternation organise the surface as a coherent structural field.

This provides the conceptual bridge toward the next technological stage: the pixel-based raster of digital image systems.

Chapter 4

The Structural Colour Theory of Digital Image Systems

Digital Image Raster Pixel

From Textile Grid to Digital Raster

The structural logic of textile patterning finds a technological continuation in the digital raster. The woven network and the digital screen are not materially identical, but both construct complex visual configurations from elementary units.

In the textile, intersections between threads establish the structural field. In the digital image, the pixel assumes an analogous organisational role.

Pixel Coordinates Colour

The Pixel as Structural Unit

A pixel has no autonomous pictorial meaning. Its perceptual function emerges through its position and its relationship with surrounding pixels.

Digital representation therefore transforms the image into a structured field of discrete positions, each carrying spatial and chromatic information.

The visual whole emerges from the regulated interaction of these elementary units.

RGB Colour Space

Quantified Colour

Digital imaging replaces the material properties of pigments and threads with numerical colour values.

Within the RGB model, colour is represented through combinations of red, green and blue light. The image can therefore be described as a numerical structure rather than only as a material surface.

This transition makes colour directly available to mathematical operations and computational transformation.

Pointillism Subpixels

From Dots to Subpixels

The book identifies a structural relationship between Neo-Impressionist pointillism and the discrete structure of digital displays.

Both rely upon the viewer's perception to integrate discrete colour elements into a larger visual experience.

The physical mechanisms differ: pigment-based optical mixing reduces brightness, whereas additive RGB light can produce highly luminous colours.

The digital display can therefore be interpreted as a technological continuation of the theoretical principle explored by pointillist painting.

Digital Imaging as Structural Continuity

The painted dot, the modular textile unit, the discrete control mechanism of the Jacquard system and the pixel structure of digital imaging can be understood as successive stages in a historical chain.

Each stage converts visual organisation into increasingly explicit systems of discrete elements and relationships.

In this perspective, Delaunay's Simultanism becomes particularly significant because its relational colour logic anticipates a form of visual organisation that can eventually be expressed mathematically.

Chapter 5

The Algorithmic Interpretation of Simultanism and Generative Art

Generative Art Rules

From Composition to System

From the perspective of contemporary generative art, Sonia Delaunay's work can be interpreted as a formal system whose internal relationships can be translated into explicit rules.

Recurring relationships between colours, geometric elements and compositional rhythm form structural invariants that are not dependent on a single physical artwork.

The artwork can therefore be understood not only as an object, but also as the realisation of an underlying system.

Processing p5.js

Translating Simultanism into Code

Software environments such as Processing and p5.js make it possible to express visual relationships as mathematical operations and programming instructions.

Complementary relationships can be represented through logical conditions, while repetition and rhythm can be generated through loops, iterations and parameterised functions.

Geometric modules can be stored in arrays and data structures, allowing new configurations to emerge while preserving the underlying principles of the composition.

Generative Systems Parameters

The Program as Artwork

A traditional painting exists as a materially fixed configuration. A generative artwork instead stores the rules through which visual states can be produced.

Each execution may generate a new configuration while maintaining the structural identity of the system.

The emphasis consequently moves from the finished image toward the mechanism capable of producing a potentially open series of images.

Noise Perlin Simplex

Organic Variation Through Mathematics

The book considers mathematical noise functions such as Perlin noise and Simplex noise as possible mechanisms for modelling continuous variation.

Unlike simple sequences of unrelated random values, noise functions create correlated fields in which neighbouring values change smoothly.

This makes them suitable for generating complex but coherent variation similar to the subtle irregularities found in manually produced colour fields.

Flow Fields Vectors

Colour in Motion

Flow fields introduce directed vectors into the visual system. Each position receives a direction that can influence the movement and curvature of visual elements.

Colour bands and geometric structures can therefore behave as components moving through an invisible network of mathematical forces.

The resulting composition can remain dynamically unstable while preserving an underlying structural coherence.

GPU GLSL Shaders

Simultanism and Parallel Computation

The relational organisation of colour finds a technological parallel in GPU based image generation.

Instead of constructing the visual field through a strictly sequential series of operations, graphics processors perform vast numbers of calculations in parallel.

Fragment shaders written in GLSL can calculate colour values directly from spatial coordinates, vectors, trigonometric functions, temporal parameters and other mathematical transformations.

UV Coordinates Mathematical Surface

The Computational Image

A normalised UV coordinate system can describe the entire image plane as a unified mathematical surface.

Within shader-based generation, every pixel becomes a computational position. Its colour is derived from the current state of a mathematical system rather than from a pre-existing pictorial element.

This gives the relational principle of Simultanism a new technological form: every colour value emerges from a network of relationships operating throughout the visual field.

Generative Colour as Process

The final chromatic state of a generative image can emerge from the interaction of multiple mathematical functions.

Noise, flow direction, temporal parameters and neighbouring colour fields can all influence the resulting hue and intensity.

The image is consequently transformed from a predetermined object into a continuously computed visual event.

Conclusion

From Physical Light to Generative Image

A Historical Continuum

The investigation traces a continuous development from Newton's decomposition of white light, through relational colour theory and Neo-Impressionism, toward the Simultanism of Sonia Delaunay.

The same structural principle then appears in textile organisation, modular pattern systems and digital raster architectures.

These developments can therefore be understood as successive transformations of a common idea: colour is not merely an attribute applied to form, but an organising relation capable of generating visual structure.

Colour as an Autonomous Force

In Delaunay's work, colour abandons its exclusively descriptive function. It no longer exists primarily to identify objects or reproduce the visible world.

Instead, chromatic relationships generate movement, rhythm, spatial tension and visual experience.

The composition becomes an open system in which every element participates in the transformation of the whole.

From Matter to Information

Pigments, threads and physical surfaces are progressively replaced by quantified colour values, pixels, coordinate systems and mathematical operations.

The visual phenomenon becomes increasingly describable as information and executable process.

In shader-based systems, colour is calculated in real time, making the image an event produced through computation rather than merely a fixed stored object.

The Generative Principle

The central conclusion is that the development from Newtonian optics to contemporary generative imagery can be understood as the gradual realisation of the same aesthetic idea.

Colour develops from a property of matter and light into a relational perceptual phenomenon, then into an autonomous compositional principle, and finally into a mathematically executable organisational system.

Within this trajectory, Sonia Delaunay's Simultanism can be read as an analogue prototype of generative visual thinking.
