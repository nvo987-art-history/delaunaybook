INTRODUCTION

Colour is one of the most continuous phenomena in human visual culture.

It appears simultaneously as a physical phenomenon of light, a perceptual experience, an artistic medium and, in contemporary systems, a form of digital information.

The study follows the transformation of colour through these different environments. Its trajectory begins with the scientific investigation of light and perception and continues through modernist painting and textile design.

At the centre of this development is Sonia Delaunay's understanding of simultaneous colour relationships. Colour becomes increasingly independent of representation and begins to operate as an organising force in its own right.

The later chapters follow this logic into modular structures, digital raster systems and computational approaches to image generation.

CHAPTER 01

The Scientific Foundations

Modern colour theory begins with investigations into light, perception and the relationships between colours.

NEWTON / GOETHE / CHEVREUL

Colour moves from a property of light toward a phenomenon of perception and relationship.

Isaac Newton's experiments with the prism demonstrated that white light could be separated into a continuous spectrum of colours.

Goethe approached colour from another direction, concentrating on the experience of colour and the role of the perceiving observer.

Michel Eugène Chevreul's investigations of simultaneous contrast established the importance of neighbouring colours. A colour could appear differently depending on the colours surrounding it.

These developments established a relational understanding of colour. Perceived colour could no longer be treated as an isolated property.

From Physics to Perception

Colour becomes relational.

The historical development from Newton through Goethe and Chevreul establishes the conceptual conditions for colour to become an autonomous organising principle.

CHAPTER 02

Simultanism and the Autonomy of Colour

The relational character of colour becomes an autonomous principle of composition in the work of Sonia Delaunay.

SONIA DELAUNAY

Colour no longer serves only to describe an object. It becomes an active force within the composition.

Within the development of Orphism and Simultanism, Sonia Delaunay explored colour as a dynamic relationship between neighbouring forms and surfaces.

Simultaneous contrasts could create visual movement without depending on conventional perspective or representational imagery.

Colours activate one another through juxtaposition. The resulting image is therefore not simply a collection of coloured forms, but a system of relationships.

This principle allowed colour, geometry and rhythm to operate together as an autonomous visual structure.

Simultanism

Colour creates movement.

In Delaunay's visual language, relationships between colours generate rhythm, tension and a sense of movement across the picture plane.

1912–1913

Contrastes simultanés transforms colour theory into a compositional system.

In the series, contrasting colour fields are placed into direct relationship. Their visual effects depend upon adjacency rather than isolated colour values.

Geometric forms support these chromatic relationships, allowing colour to generate visual rhythm and spatial tension.

The composition consequently becomes a field of interaction in which each colour participates in the appearance of its neighbours.

1913

Bal Bullier

Colour, rhythm and movement become inseparable from the experience of modern urban life.

RHYTHM

Bal Bullier translates the atmosphere of a modern dance hall into an active field of colour and movement.

Music, artificial light, dance and bodily movement are represented through interrelated colour structures rather than through conventional narrative representation.

Curved forms and successive colour relationships produce a sensation of rotation and continuous visual flow.

1914 · Prismes électriques

Light becomes structure.

Concentric circles, arcs and chromatic segments create a modular system in which colour, geometry and rhythm operate together.

CHAPTER 03

Structural Systems of Colour

Colour moves from the pictorial field into textile structures, modular patterns and systems of production.

TEXTILE

The principles of Simultanism extend beyond painting into material and textile structures.

Textile design introduces a different physical environment in which colour operates through threads, surfaces, repetition and pattern.

Sonia Delaunay's textile work allowed simultaneous colour relationships to become mobile structures. Folds, seams, surfaces and the movement of the body continually changed the relationships between colours.

Geometric units such as bands, diamonds, zigzags and arcs could be repeated and varied to create rhythmic compositions.

STRUCTURE

Pattern as a System

MODULARITY

Repetition does not necessarily mean mechanical duplication. A repeated unit can change through colour, orientation, scale or position.

The resulting textile surface becomes a structured field in which individual elements gain their significance through their relationships with neighbouring elements.

This modular principle provides an important conceptual bridge toward later systems of digital image construction.

1804 · Jacquard Loom

Pattern becomes instruction.

The Jacquard system demonstrated how complex textile patterns could be produced through encoded sequences of operations.

CHAPTER 04

The Structural Colour Theory of Digital Image Systems

The modular logic of textile and pattern systems finds a technological continuation in the digital image.

DIGITAL RASTER

The pixel becomes the elementary unit of the computational image.

The digital raster organises an image as a structured field of discrete positions. Each position carries numerical information that contributes to the visual whole.

This creates a structural relationship with earlier systems of textile organisation. The material mechanisms are different, but both depend upon elementary units arranged according to a larger pattern.

In the digital environment, colour can be represented numerically and therefore becomes directly available to mathematical operations.

DIGITAL COLOUR

The Pixel as Structural Unit

RGB

Digital colour transforms the chromatic field into numerical information.

Within the RGB model, red, green and blue components are combined to produce colours on a digital display.

A digital image can therefore be described as a collection of spatial positions carrying colour values.

The visual result is not determined by one pixel alone. It emerges through the relationships among many neighbouring values across the raster.

Optical Mixing

From dots to pixels.

The book connects the discrete colour elements of Neo-Impressionist painting with the structured colour elements of digital displays, while recognising the fundamental physical difference between pigment and emitted light.

CONTINUITY

The painted dot, the modular textile element and the digital pixel can be considered successive forms of discrete visual organisation.

Each system constructs larger visual structures from elementary components whose relationships determine the appearance of the whole.

The digital raster makes this structural principle explicitly computational: visual information can now be processed, transformed and generated through mathematical operations.

CHAPTER 05

The Algorithmic Interpretation

The principles of Simultanism are considered from the perspective of generative and computational image systems.

GENERATIVE ART

A visual system can become a set of rules capable of producing images.

The relational principles visible in Delaunay's work can be considered as structures that may be translated into explicit computational relationships.

Colour relationships, geometric repetition, rhythm and variation can become parameters within a generative system.

The emphasis consequently shifts from a single predetermined image toward the rules through which a potentially open series of images can be produced.

ALGORITHMIC

Colour as Process

RULES

Programming environments allow relationships between colour and geometry to be expressed through parameters, functions, iteration and mathematical operations.

A repeated geometric structure can be modified according to rules governing position, colour, scale or orientation.

The resulting image is therefore the visible outcome of a system rather than simply a fixed collection of manually placed elements.

Perlin / Simplex Noise

Structure with variation.

Mathematical noise functions can introduce continuous variation into generated fields while maintaining relationships between neighbouring values.

FLOW FIELDS

Mathematical direction can become a visual force.

Flow fields assign directions to positions within a visual space. Elements can then respond to these vectors and move through the field.

Such systems allow visual movement to emerge from mathematical relationships rather than from manually determined paths.

GPU / GLSL

The Computational Image

Shader-based systems make it possible to calculate colour directly across the image surface.

SHADERS

Every pixel can become the result of a mathematical calculation.

In GPU-based image generation, a fragment shader can calculate colour values from coordinates, mathematical functions and other parameters.

The image is therefore not necessarily stored as a finished visual object. It can be generated continuously from the rules of the system.

This provides a computational form of relational colour: the appearance of each point is determined within a larger mathematical field.

CONCLUSION

From Physical Light to Generative Image

The study closes the historical trajectory by connecting colour, structure, perception and computation.

CONTINUUM

Colour develops from a property of light into a relational and generative principle.

The trajectory begins with Newton's investigation of light and continues through Goethe's study of perception, Chevreul's theory of simultaneous contrast and the Neo-Impressionist application of colour theory.

Sonia Delaunay's Simultanism marks a decisive transformation. Colour becomes increasingly independent from representation and functions as an autonomous organising force.

The same structural logic can then be followed through textile systems, modular patterns and the technological organisation of digital images.

In computational systems, colour can finally be described through numerical relationships and generated through explicit rules.

Colour is not merely applied to form. It can become the principle through which form itself is generated.
